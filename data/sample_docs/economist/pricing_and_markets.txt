Dynamic and personalized pricing lets a seller set different prices for different
buyers based on observed willingness to pay, rather than one posted price for
everyone. Classic price discrimination theory splits this into three degrees:
first-degree (a unique price per buyer, the theoretical maximum extraction of
consumer surplus), second-degree (menus like bulk discounts or tiered plans,
where buyers self-select), and third-degree (different prices for different
observable groups, like student discounts).

Economically, perfect first-degree discrimination is efficient in the narrow
sense that it maximizes the number of transactions that occur -- every buyer
whose valuation exceeds marginal cost gets served -- but it transfers nearly
all the surplus from consumer to producer. The efficiency gain and the
distributional harm happen at the same time, which is why the same policy can
be defended as welfare-maximizing and attacked as extractive depending on which
metric you weight.

AI-driven personalized pricing approximates first-degree discrimination using
behavioral and demographic signals as proxies for willingness to pay. The
economic risk isn't just "consumers pay more" -- it's that pricing becomes
opaque and unpredictable, which itself has a cost: buyers can no longer
comparison-shop effectively, undermining the price-discovery function markets
are supposed to serve.

Minimum wage increases raise the cost of the cheapest labor inputs. Standard
competitive-market theory predicts reduced employment among low-wage workers;
empirical labor economics over the last three decades (notably the Card-Krueger
natural experiments) has found much smaller or near-zero disemployment effects
in many real-world cases, which is now attributed to employer monopsony power
in local labor markets rather than the textbook competitive model. The honest
answer is that the effect size is empirically contested and depends heavily on
local labor market structure, not settled either direction.

Tariffs raise the domestic price of an imported good by roughly the tariff
amount in the short run, though the split between "consumers pay more" and
"foreign producers absorb margin" depends on the price elasticity of demand
and supply on both sides. Tariffs protect the specific domestic industry being
shielded, at a broadly distributed cost to consumers and downstream industries
that use the tariffed good as an input.
