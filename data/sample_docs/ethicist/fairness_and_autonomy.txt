Consequentialist frameworks judge an action by its outcomes: personalized AI
pricing is justified if it increases total welfare (more transactions occur,
some buyers pay less than the old flat price), even if it makes some buyers
worse off, as long as aggregate benefit outweighs aggregate harm. The
weakness of this framing is that it can justify concentrating harm on a
vulnerable minority as long as the majority benefits, which is precisely the
scenario personalized pricing risks: it tends to charge more to buyers with
fewer alternatives, who are often already disadvantaged.

Deontological frameworks ask whether the action itself, independent of
outcome, violates a duty or a right -- most relevantly here, a duty of
transparency and a right against being manipulated using information the
buyer didn't knowingly share. Charging someone a higher price based on
inferred desperation or lack of alternatives, without disclosure, arguably
violates informed consent even if no explicit deception occurred, because
consent requires the buyer to understand the terms they're accepting.

Fairness in the Rawlsian sense asks: would you accept this pricing system if
you didn't know in advance which side of it you'd be on? Personalized pricing
that systematically extracts more from lower-income or less price-savvy
buyers fails this test, because a rational person behind the "veil of
ignorance" would not choose a system that could disadvantage them without
their knowledge.

On surveillance and monitoring: the core ethical tension is proportionality --
whether the harm prevented justifies the autonomy and privacy cost imposed on
everyone being monitored, including the overwhelming majority who did nothing
wrong. Justified surveillance regimes typically require narrow scope, time
limits, oversight independent of the party doing the surveilling, and a
articulated threat the monitoring is proportionate to. Absent those
constraints, surveillance tends to expand past its original justification --
this is sometimes called mission creep, and it is one of the most
well-documented failure modes in applied ethics of monitoring systems.
