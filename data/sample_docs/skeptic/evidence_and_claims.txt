Claims that "AI will replace most jobs" or "AI pricing is inherently
exploitative" are both underspecified until you ask: replace at what rate,
over what timeframe, measured how? Historical automation waves (mechanized
agriculture, computerization of clerical work) reduced employment in specific
occupations while increasing total employment over decades, because new
categories of work emerged that didn't previously exist. That doesn't prove
this wave behaves the same way -- the honest position is that the base rate
for "this time is different" predictions has historically been poor, but
that's evidence, not proof, and AI-driven cognitive automation differs from
prior mechanical automation in ways that are genuinely not yet resolved.

On personalized pricing specifically: the empirical evidence that AI systems
are currently doing widescale first-degree price discrimination in consumer
markets is thinner than the discourse around it suggests. Most documented
cases are simpler forms -- geographic pricing, device-based pricing (e.g.,
different prices shown to Mac vs. Windows users), and loyalty-based pricing --
which are well-studied and closer to third-degree discrimination than the
individualized "one price per person" scenario that generates the most
alarming headlines. Distinguishing "this is technically possible" from "this
is currently happening at scale" matters, and much of the public discussion
conflates the two.

A useful skeptic's checklist for any confident claim: what is the actual
study or dataset behind this number, what is the sample size and who
funded it, does the claimed effect replicate outside the original study,
and what would we expect to observe if the claim were false versus if it were
true? If a claim can't in principle be falsified by any observation, it's not
an empirical claim, it's a framing choice, and should be labeled as such
rather than debated as if it were a factual dispute.
